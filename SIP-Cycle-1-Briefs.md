# SIP Cycle 1 Briefs

*Atria Xcelerator — Social Impact Programme*

Twenty-six problems from seven organisations. Each one is real, each one has someone waiting for an answer, and each one is open enough that many teams can approach it many different ways. Read the brief, then tell the sponsor how you would solve it.

Some facts in these briefs are not settled yet. Where a brief tells you to find something out, that is work rather than an oversight.

Written to the plain-English rules published by the UK Government Digital Service: short sentences, everyday words, active voice, and the important thing first.

## Brief 01 — Opportunity Navigator

**Sponsor:** Dream a Dream  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · ~10 teams

> A young person knows what they want. They do not know what to do on Monday morning. Close that gap.

### 1. Who you would work with

Dream a Dream is a Bengaluru non-profit that works with children and young people from vulnerable backgrounds. Its method is life skills and social-emotional learning, not academic coaching. It runs Thriving Centres for young people, works inside government schools, and has state hubs across India. It points out that 54% of India’s young people are not ready for work.

### 2. The situation

A young person at a Thriving Centre wants a job, a course, or money to study. The information they need already exists. It is spread across dozens of websites, in more than one language, written for people who already understand the system. Government schemes for education, jobs, skills, business and financial support sit on separate central and state portals, each with its own rules and forms.

### 3. What is going wrong

Young people find opportunities through people they already know. Their networks are small, so their options are small. Someone with an uncle in a bank hears about bank jobs. Someone without one hears about nothing.

Dream a Dream describes the failure as a sentence: young people can say “I am interested in this” but cannot say “I know what I can do next”. Interest does not turn into a step.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Job portals exist and are useless here. They assume a degree, a resume, a stable internet connection, and enough English to read a listing. They rank by employer need, not by what a young person could realistically get. Scholarship aggregators go stale within a year because the deadlines move and nobody maintains them.

The hard part is not collecting listings. It is judging which of them a specific person can actually get, and saying so honestly.

### 5. What already exists

Dream a Dream runs a career awareness approach today, and asks you to go beyond it rather than repeat it.

Ask them what that approach covers, what tools the facilitators use, and what has already been tried and dropped. Do that before you design anything.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- public government scheme portals and their data, where it is published
- access to Thriving Centre facilitators and young people for interviews, subject to Dream a Dream’s consent process
- any opportunity list, spreadsheet or partner feed Dream a Dream already keeps — ask whether it can be shared

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A working tool that a facilitator can sit down with a young person and use. It has to do three things: find opportunities the young person would not have found alone, say plainly whether they are eligible and what it would take, and end with a next step specific enough to act on this week. It must work for someone reading in Kannada as comfortably as in English, and on a low-end Android phone.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- no scraped personal data about young people leaves Dream a Dream’s control
- the tool must not invent an opportunity, a deadline or an eligibility rule — if you cannot source it, say so on screen
- it must be usable by a facilitator with no technical training, on a phone, offline for at least the reading parts

### 9. Finding your approach

Your first idea will probably be everyone else’s. Before you commit to it, find out what already exists in this space and why none of it has solved the problem for a young person at a Thriving Centre.

Then answer the questions a good approach has to answer: who keeps this accurate in month seven, how do you prove an eligibility claim is right, and what does the tool do when the honest answer is that this person is not eligible for anything yet?

### 10. Your sponsor

Dream a Dream.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT. Some room for design-minded students from any branch.
- **Technical skills** — Data collection and cleaning, search and ranking, retrieval over documents, multilingual interfaces, low-bandwidth front-end work.
- **Other skills** — User interviews, writing plainly for a reader who is not you, judging a source’s reliability.

## Brief 02 — Community Asset and Youth Mapping

**Sponsor:** Dream a Dream  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Data · ~10 teams

> A Thriving Centre does not know who lives around it or what is already there. Draw the map.

### 1. Who you would work with

Dream a Dream, as described in Brief 01. This problem sits with the team that runs the Thriving Centres and decides who the centre should be reaching.

### 2. The situation

A Thriving Centre sits in a neighbourhood. Within 8 to 10 kilometres of it there are young people who have left school, young people about to leave, employers, training institutes, clinics, libraries, sports grounds, self-help groups and small businesses. Some of that is recorded in census data, school records and government datasets. Most of it is in nobody’s records at all.

### 3. What is going wrong

The centre reaches the young people who walk in. It does not know how many it is missing, or where they are. Outreach is guesswork.

The same blindness applies to what is around the centre. A tailoring unit two streets away that would take an apprentice, or a college with a free evening class, is invisible unless a staff member happens to know about it.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Indian secondary data on young people is patchy, out of date, and published at the wrong level. District figures do not tell you about one neighbourhood. School dropout data exists but is held by different departments in different formats. Mapping community assets by hand takes weeks per neighbourhood and goes stale.

### 5. What already exists

Ask Dream a Dream what mapping they have already tried, and what data they hold on the young people currently attending. Do not assume the answer is nothing. Finding out is the first piece of work.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- published secondary data: census, education department records, state dashboards, open map data
- the location of at least one Thriving Centre as your test site
- possible fieldwork in that neighbourhood, subject to Dream a Dream’s consent process

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A map of one real neighbourhood around one real centre, plus the method you used to make it. The map has to answer two questions a centre manager will actually ask: which young people near us are we not reaching, and what is already here that we could use. The method matters as much as the map, because Dream a Dream needs to repeat this for the next centre without you.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- every figure on the map carries its source and the year it refers to
- where data does not exist, the map says so rather than estimating
- no identifiable information about any young person appears in anything you hand over
- no household is surveyed door to door unless Dream a Dream is running the consent process

### 9. Finding your approach

Around nine other teams are reading this same brief. Assume your first instinct is also theirs, and go looking for what makes the problem hard rather than what makes a demonstration look good.

Two questions worth arriving with: how do you estimate a population you cannot see, from public data that was never collected for this purpose, and what would make a centre manager still be updating your map a year after you have gone?

### 10. Your sponsor

Dream a Dream.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT. Strong fit for anyone interested in data science or geospatial work.
- **Technical skills** — Public data sourcing, geospatial analysis and mapping, estimation from incomplete data, data visualisation, survey design.
- **Other skills** — Judging source quality, stating uncertainty honestly, documenting a method someone else can repeat.

## Brief 03 — Alumni and Mentor Network

**Sponsor:** Dream a Dream  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · ~10 teams

> Young people leave the Thriving Centre and disappear. Some of them are exactly who the next group needs to meet.

### 1. Who you would work with

Dream a Dream, as described in Brief 01. This problem covers three of their questions at once: staying connected to alumni, helping young people learn from each other, and connecting them to people who can open doors.

### 2. The situation

Young people spend time at a Thriving Centre, build skills and confidence, and then leave. Some go into work, some into further study, some into neither. The centre loses touch with almost all of them.

Meanwhile the young people still at the centre need two things the centre cannot supply on its own: someone slightly ahead of them who has done the thing they are trying to do, and someone outside their world who can make an introduction.

### 3. What is going wrong

The alumni are the answer to the mentoring problem and nobody can find them. Phone numbers change. There is no reason to stay in touch, so nobody does.

Dream a Dream is explicit that the connection has to be worth something to the alumni too. A network that only asks past participants for favours will empty out.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Alumni networks fail everywhere, including at wealthy universities with full-time staff running them. They fail for one reason: people join, nothing happens, they stop opening the emails. Building the database is a week of work. Making anyone come back is the actual problem, and it is a design problem more than a technical one.

### 5. What already exists

An alumni contact list exists. It is unstructured and scattered, so expect to spend real time making it usable before you can do anything with it.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- access to current Thriving Centre participants and facilitators for interviews, subject to consent
- access to alumni themselves, whose behaviour you most need to understand — reaching them is part of the work
- a pilot with a real group during your cycle is possible. Whether it happens is up to you to convince Dream a Dream

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

Something real that a group of young people used, with evidence of whether they came back. A design document is not enough. Even a small pilot with twenty people over three weeks, with honest numbers about how many returned after the first session, is worth more than a polished app nobody opened.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- contact details of young people stay under Dream a Dream’s control, not yours and not a third party’s
- no young person is matched with an outside adult without Dream a Dream’s safeguarding process in between
- the mechanism must give the alumni something, not only ask them for something

### 9. Finding your approach

Alumni networks and mentor-matching schemes have been built many times, by organisations with far more money than Dream a Dream, and most of them are dead. Find out why before you design yours.

One question decides this project: why does anyone open it a second time? If you cannot answer that in a sentence, you do not have a proposal yet.

### 10. Your sponsor

Dream a Dream.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT. Suits students who want product and behaviour work rather than heavy engineering.
- **Technical skills** — Mobile-first and messaging-based interfaces, lightweight backend, matching logic, privacy-aware data handling.
- **Other skills** — Behavioural design, running a pilot and measuring it honestly, interviewing, safeguarding awareness.

## Brief 04 — Youth Ownership of the Centre

**Sponsor:** Dream a Dream  
**Tags:** Mech · Non-tech · Physical build · ~10 teams

> The Thriving Centre is run for young people. Dream a Dream wants it run by them. Design the space and the rules that make that happen.

### 1. Who you would work with

Dream a Dream, as described in Brief 01. This problem joins two of their questions: designing spaces that support the Thriving journey outside scheduled sessions, and designing spaces that invite young people to change them.

### 2. The situation

A Thriving Centre runs structured sessions at set times. Outside those hours the building is a building. Dream a Dream believes the important things — friendship, experimenting, belonging — happen in the unstructured time, and that the young people should be shaping the place, not visiting it.

### 3. What is going wrong

A room designed by adults for a programme signals who it belongs to. Fixed furniture, locked cupboards and finished walls all say: this is ours, you are a guest. Young people behave accordingly.

Dream a Dream’s words for the shift they want are moving young people “from participants to co-creators”. Right now the physical space works against that.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Handing over a space sounds easy and is not. Someone still has to be responsible when something breaks, when two groups want the same room, or when a decision goes badly. Most attempts collapse into either adults quietly taking control back, or a free-for-all that staff then have to clean up. The design has to survive both.

### 5. What already exists

Floor plans, photographs, an inventory of what is in the building and the terms it is held on may all exist somewhere. Finding out what exists, and getting hold of it, is your job.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- site visits to at least one Thriving Centre, which you arrange yourselves
- the young people who use it, as co-designers rather than research subjects
- no materials budget at present. Anything you propose has to be cheap enough that the answer could change

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

Something built and installed in a real centre, plus the rules that go with it. Both halves are required. A modular wall that young people can rearrange is useless without an answer to who decides, who fixes it, and what happens when it gets broken. Bring evidence that young people actually changed something after you left.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- safe for children and young people, including when misused
- repairable by centre staff with ordinary tools and locally bought parts
- reversible — nothing that permanently damages a rented building
- designed with young people, not for them, with evidence of how they shaped it

### 9. Finding your approach

Furniture is the easy half. The harder half is that a room designed by adults signals who it belongs to, and no amount of soft seating changes that.

The stronger position deals with governance as well as objects: what are young people actually allowed to decide, how is that decision made and recorded, and what stops staff quietly overruling it?

### 10. Your sponsor

Dream a Dream.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — Mechanical, and any branch with an interest in design. One of the few problems in this pool with a physical build at its centre.
- **Technical skills** — Fabrication with basic tools, modular and flat-pack design, material selection and costing, safety assessment, prototyping.
- **Other skills** — Participatory design, facilitation, writing rules people will actually follow.

## Brief 05 — Physical Education for Grades 1 to 10

**Sponsor:** Dream a Dream  
**Tags:** Mech · Non-tech · Physical build · ~10 teams

> PE in a government school is usually a ball, a field, and the same six children playing. Make it work for the other thirty.

### 1. Who you would work with

Dream a Dream, as described in Brief 01. This problem sits inside their Thriving Schools programme, which works in government schools alongside the state curriculum.

### 2. The situation

Physical education is on the timetable from Grade 1 to Grade 10. One teacher takes a class that spans a wide range of ages, sizes, confidence levels and physical abilities. Equipment is limited. The state curriculum sets what must be covered.

### 3. What is going wrong

Most PE sessions are one game, played by the children who are already good at it. The rest stand at the edge. A child who is smaller, slower, less confident, or disabled gets less activity, not more, which is the reverse of what PE is for.

Dream a Dream wants sessions that are inclusive, engaging and right for the age group, across a ten-grade range.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

The teacher is the bottleneck and cannot be replaced. Anything that requires a PE specialist, a lot of setup, or careful supervision of small groups will not survive contact with a real school day. Equipment that needs storing, charging or repairing disappears within a term.

### 5. What already exists

The state curriculum for physical education, which your work must align to rather than replace.

Which state, which curriculum document, what equipment the schools already have, and what Dream a Dream runs there now. Find all of that out early, because it shapes everything you design.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- access to a school, a class and a PE teacher for testing. You will have to hustle for it
- class size, ground size and the current equipment list. Ask Dream a Dream when you meet them — these are good scoping questions

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

Something tested with real children in a real school, with evidence about who participated. The strongest measure is simple: count how many children in the class were physically active for how long, before and after. That single number is worth more than any amount of description.

Your output can be equipment, a set of activities, a way of grouping children, or a combination. It must work for a teacher with no specialist training.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- safe for children, including unsupervised misuse
- aligned to the state curriculum, not a replacement for it
- cheap enough to put in many schools, and repairable locally
- inclusive by design, including for children with disabilities — not a separate activity for them

### 9. Finding your approach

Be clear about what you are actually solving. The problem is not a shortage of equipment. It is that six children dominate any game with a ball in it while thirty stand at the edge.

Whatever you propose will be judged against one measure: how many children were physically active, and for how long. Work backwards from that number.

### 10. Your sponsor

Dream a Dream, Thriving Schools programme.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — Mechanical, and any branch interested in design or sport. Little software content.
- **Technical skills** — Low-cost product design, fabrication, materials and durability, safety testing, designing for measurement.
- **Other skills** — Working with teachers and children, observation and measurement, inclusive design, curriculum reading.

## Brief 06 — Finding a Child’s Real Learning Level

**Sponsor:** Project SparkL  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · ~10 teams

> A child in Grade 6 who reads like a Grade 2 child needs to be taught at Grade 2. First you have to know.

### 1. Who you would work with

Project SparkL is a Bengaluru trust, registered in 2022, that teaches foundational reading and arithmetic to children from vulnerable backgrounds, including children living in shelter homes. It runs after-school evening sessions at seven centres for about 130 to 140 children, with two partner organisations.

### 2. The situation

The children SparkL works with are enrolled in a school grade that does not match what they can actually do. A child may be registered in Grade 6 and be unable to read a Grade 2 sentence.

SparkL already handles this properly. It assesses each child in Kannada, Maths and English across grade levels, works out a “skill grade”, and builds a learning plan from that rather than from the school grade. This is the core of their method and it works.

### 3. What is going wrong

The method is manual, so it stops at seven centres. Assessing each child, working out the skill grade, planning the teaching and tracking progress all depend on a small number of people doing it by hand.

SparkL wants to reach more children. Right now growth means hiring proportionally, which is the definition of a model that will not scale.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Off-the-shelf learning apps assume the child’s grade is their level, which is precisely the assumption that fails here. Adaptive learning products assume a smartphone per child, reliable internet and a literate parent nearby. None of those hold in a shelter home in the evening.

There is also a harder problem. Assessing a child in three languages, including Kannada, without a trained adult listening to them read, is genuinely difficult.

### 5. What already exists

SparkL’s own assessment approach and skill-grade system. Do not replace it — understand it first, then work out which parts a machine can carry. Facilitator training is a separate problem and is covered by Brief 19; coordinate with whoever takes it, because you are both working on how SparkL grows past seven centres.

The assessment materials, how the skill grade is worked out, and what records SparkL keeps today may all be available. Ask for them.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- sample assessment records, anonymised progress data and session plans, if you can persuade SparkL to share them
- a session at a centre to sit in on, if you arrange it. Watching one is worth more than any document
- the educators who run the sessions, who know more about this than any document will tell you

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

One part of the loop, working, tested with real records. That could be the assessment itself, the skill-grade calculation, the learning plan, or the progress tracking. Choose one and make it good enough that SparkL would use it next term.

It must work in Kannada as well as English, on a shared low-end device, and without assuming internet during the session.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- no identifiable data about any child leaves SparkL’s control, and none goes into a third-party service without written agreement — several of these children are in shelter homes
- Kannada is not an afterthought or a translated label — the assessment has to work in it
- works offline during a session, syncs later
- an educator with no technical training can run it

### 9. Finding your approach

Understand SparkL’s existing method properly before you automate any part of it. The educators have already solved problems you have not thought of.

Then two questions decide whether SparkL could use what you build. How do you assess a child’s reading without an adult listening to them? And what happens on the evening the internet is down and the tablet is at 8%?

### 10. Your sponsor

Project SparkL.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT.
- **Technical skills** — Offline-first application design, speech or audio processing for reading assessment, adaptive logic, multilingual and Indic-script interfaces, data privacy.
- **Other skills** — Working with educators, assessment design, writing for children, understanding a method before automating it.

## Brief 07 — Two-Wheeler Technician Training

**Sponsor:** Project SparkL  
**Tags:** Mech · EC · Cap at ~4 teams

> India’s scooters are going electric. The people who repair them were trained on petrol engines. Build the 60 hours that closes the gap.

### 1. Who you would work with

Project SparkL, described in Brief 06. This problem comes from Project Scale Up, their work on making the organisation financially sustainable rather than grant-dependent.

### 2. The situation

SparkL wants to run a 60-hour hands-on training programme for young people aged 18 to 25 from ITI and Polytechnic backgrounds. It covers petrol vehicles, electric vehicles, scooters and motorcycles, and needs workshop infrastructure and practical exposure to work at all.

SparkL has also identified a second gap: young people finish formal qualifications without the workplace skills that get them hired — communication, spreadsheets, presentations, email.

### 3. What is going wrong

An ITI or Polytechnic certificate does not make someone employable in a two-wheeler workshop. The training is theory-heavy and the equipment is often a generation behind the vehicles on the road. Electric two-wheelers are the sharpest version of this: battery packs, controllers, motor diagnostics and high-voltage safety are absent from most curricula.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Hands-on training is expensive. It needs vehicles, tools, space and a trainer per small group. Manufacturer training programmes exist but are closed to their own dealer networks. Sixty hours is a real constraint: it is enough to make someone useful at a narrow set of tasks and nowhere near enough to make a general technician.

### 5. What already exists

Atria University and Atria Institute of Technology have an e-mobility department, which currently holds the Atria Foundation food truck for an electric powertrain conversion. That department is the most obvious source of equipment, expertise and workshop space for this problem.

Whether SparkL has drafted any of the curriculum, and whether workshop space or vehicles have been secured, is for you to find out.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- workshop space, tools and vehicles to train on. Securing these is part of the project
- trainee candidates to test the material on, whom you will have to recruit
- the Atria e-mobility department, which holds the equipment and the expertise. Whether they take part is up to you to arrange

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A section of the 60 hours, built and taught to real people at least once, with evidence of what they could do afterwards that they could not do before. That means the trainer notes, the equipment list with costs, the practical exercises, and the assessment that proves competence.

An employer’s view is what matters. “Completed the module” is worth nothing. “Can diagnose a controller fault in under fifteen minutes” is worth something.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- high-voltage safety is taught and enforced before anyone touches a battery pack — this is not negotiable
- the equipment list has to be affordable for a small training centre, with current prices and dates
- the material must be teachable by a trainer who is a good mechanic, not an engineer
- assessment proves a task can be done, not that a class was attended

### 9. Finding your approach

Sixty hours is the constraint that shapes everything. The useful question is not what a technician should know, but what you can honestly teach in sixty hours that gets someone hired.

Talk to a workshop owner before you write a single line of syllabus. Ask what they actually test when somebody walks in looking for work.

### 10. Your sponsor

Project SparkL, Project Scale Up.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — Mechanical and Electronics. The strongest Mech and EC problem in this pool.
- **Technical skills** — Electric and internal combustion powertrains, battery systems, motor controllers, diagnostics, workshop practice, electrical safety, tooling and costing.
- **Other skills** — Teaching and instructional design, competence assessment, talking to employers about what they hire for.

## Brief 08 — Communications Analytics Dashboard

**Sponsor:** Centre for Wildlife Studies  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · ~10 teams

> Eight platforms, eight logins, one monthly report assembled by hand. Automate it.

### 1. Who you would work with

The Centre for Wildlife Studies is a Bengaluru research and conservation organisation, working for around four decades on wildlife research, conservation, policy and education. Its programmes include Wild Seve, Wild Shaale, Wild Surakshe and a doctoral programme. Wild Seve alone has filed over 35,000 compensation claims for more than 17,000 families in over 4,000 settlements since 2015.

This problem comes from the Media and Outreach team, and CWS ranked it their first priority.

### 2. The situation

CWS communicates and fundraises through a website, Google Analytics, Google Search Console, Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube, Flodesk for email, Razorpay for payments, and campaign activity on top. Each holds a piece of the picture. None of them talk to each other.

### 3. What is going wrong

Somebody logs into eight systems every month, copies numbers into a spreadsheet, and builds a report. It takes hours, it happens late, and it produces figures nobody can act on quickly enough to change a campaign that is failing.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

The platforms make it hard on purpose. Instagram and LinkedIn restrict their data access, YouTube and GA4 have their own models and quotas, and Flodesk and Razorpay are small enough that their access options are limited. Some of these have no usable interface for automated access at all, which means part of this job is not integration but designing an honest manual step that does not rot.

### 5. What already exists

The current monthly reporting process, whatever it is. Understand it before replacing it — the person who builds that spreadsheet knows which numbers matter and which are noise.

Ask how the monthly report is built today, which platform accounts you could be given, and whether anyone has tried to automate it before.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- read-only access to each platform, which CWS has to grant. This is the single biggest thing to settle in week one
- historical exports to build and test against, if CWS will share them
- the Media and Outreach team, who will tell you what they actually look at

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A dashboard the CWS team opens instead of building the spreadsheet. It refreshes on its own where the platform allows it, and has a clear, documented manual step where it does not. Every metric has a written definition, so two people reading it agree on what it means. It comes with a handover document, because you leave and CWS keeps it.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- no donor personal data from Razorpay or Flodesk goes anywhere it should not, including into a chart
- credentials are never stored in the code or the repository
- a person who is not a programmer must be able to keep it running after you leave
- where a number is manually entered, the dashboard says so

### 9. Finding your approach

Several teams will land on the same tool over exported spreadsheets, and it is not obviously the wrong answer. That means visuals will not separate you.

Maintenance will. Who refreshes the export, what happens when a platform renames a column, and why is your answer better than the spreadsheet it replaces? Treat this as a data engineering and handover problem rather than a charting problem.

### 10. Your sponsor

CWS Media and Outreach team. Note that this team also sponsors Brief 09, so their time is split.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT.
- **Technical skills** — Working with third-party interfaces and their limits, scheduled jobs, data modelling, dashboard and chart design, credential handling.
- **Other skills** — Defining metrics unambiguously, writing documentation someone else can follow, designing for handover.

## Brief 09 — Find Anything CWS Has

**Sponsor:** Centre for Wildlife Studies  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · ~10 teams

> Forty years of photographs, reports and hard-won numbers. No way to find any of it.

### 1. Who you would work with

CWS Media and Outreach, as described in Brief 08. This brief combines two of their requests, ranked second and third, because both are the same problem: finding what CWS already has.

### 2. The situation

Two archives. One is a large and growing set of photographs and videos in Google Drive folders. The other is a set of documents holding approved statistics, programme information and institutional facts — the numbers CWS is allowed to publish.

A staff member writing a grant application or a press response needs both, quickly, and needs to know the number is current and approved.

### 3. What is going wrong

Finding a photograph means remembering which folder it is in and who took it. Finding a statistic means asking a colleague, who may give you last year’s figure. A wrong number in a grant application or a news article is a real cost to a research organisation.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Drive search reads filenames, and a filename like IMG_4471.jpg tells you nothing. Adding metadata to thousands of existing files by hand is more work than anyone has time for. ## Brief 12 — The LoveAll Transparency Ledger

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Software · Full context pack · ~10 teams

> ₹1,000 goes in. A meal, a trained welder, a treated patient or a planted tree comes out. Prove it, rupee by rupee, without breaking four separate laws.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation is the charitable arm of Atria Group. It runs food kitchens and food trucks, the Atria Mega Skill Centre, healthcare initiatives and plantation drives. Its stated numbers include over 1.7 crore cumulative meals served, more than 4,000 people trained in trades such as tailoring, hospitality and IT, and around 620 placed with employers including KPR Mills, Arvind Mills, Hotel Leela Palace and Reliance Trends. It operates around 20 kitchens across roughly 255 locations with 200 or more volunteers.

LoveAll is its social loyalty card. Members pay ₹1,000 a year, get discounts across a network of Bengaluru restaurants and retailers, and their money funds four causes: Food, Education, Healthcare and Bio-Diversity.

### 2. The situation

LoveAll makes a promise most charities do not: that you can see exactly where your money went. The Foundation states it is moving its fund flow onto a blockchain so that transparency is coded in rather than promised.

The programme also wants to grow beyond funding Atria Foundation’s own work, and become a funding engine that other registered NGOs can plug into. That ambition is where the difficulty lives.

### 3. What is going wrong

Nothing is broken yet. The promise has been made and the system that would keep it does not exist. Right now a member who asks “what did my ₹1,000 do” gets an aggregate number, not a traceable answer.

The harder problem is that a naive ledger would be wrong in a way that matters. Indian tax law treats “we spent money on our own programme” and “we gave money to another NGO” completely differently. A ledger that reports ₹1,00,000 sent to a partner NGO as ₹1,00,000 of impact misstates the Foundation’s tax position, even when the partner genuinely spent every rupee.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Four sets of rules apply at the same time, and satisfying one while ignoring another still leaves you non-compliant:

- **Income Tax Act** — a registered charity must apply at least 85% of its income to charitable purposes each year. Since the Finance Act 2023, a general donation from one registered NGO to another counts as only 85% application for the giver. Corpus donations do not count at all
- **Companies Act, Section 135** — for corporate money to count as CSR, the receiving entity must be registered on Form CSR-1 and hold valid 12AB and 80G
- **FCRA** — since the 2020 amendment, passing foreign contributions on to any other organisation is prohibited outright. This is a hard stop, not a tax inefficiency
- **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023** — requires that personal data can be corrected and erased. A blockchain cannot easily erase anything. The main obligations are expected around May 2027

That last one is the genuine engineering tension in this problem, and nobody in the industry has solved it cleanly.

### 5. What already exists

An 18-page context brief prepared by the Foundation, covering the fund-flow map, the stakeholder map, the four pillars, the original blockchain rationale and the full compliance picture. It deliberately stops short of proposing an architecture. Read it before you pitch — this is by far the best-prepared problem in this pool.

Registrations already in place: 12AB, 80G and CSR-1. Ground-truth data already being collected includes a mandatory daily photo submission from the food trucks and field check-ins, which the brief suggests could feed attestations.

The Foundation’s technology stack document exists as a Google Doc and could not be extracted into the context brief. It likely already defines the app and database layer your work would sit alongside, so ask for it early.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- the 18-page LoveAll context brief, including four diagrams: the fund-flow map, the four-pillar structure, a conceptual attestation pipeline, and the compliance layers
- Atria Institute of Technology and Atria University, named in the brief as a possible source of technical and build support
- the technology stack document, sample transaction data and the current app or database, all of which you will have to ask for

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A working prototype of one part of the pipeline, on simulated payments, that a compliance-minded reader would accept. The context brief names five building blocks worth prototyping:

- a member ledger that recognises donors without putting their personal data on a chain
- a fund pool where every inflow is tagged Food, Education, Healthcare, Bio-Diversity or Operations at the point of entry
- a disbursement contract that releases funds only when a real-world event is attested — a delivery photo, a placement record, a plantation record
- a routing module for transfers to partner NGOs that carries the compliance metadata with it
- a public dashboard showing aggregated figures without exposing personal data

Pick one or two. Make the compliance behaviour real, not commented out.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- no raw personal data on any chain, public or permissioned — hashes, pseudonymous identifiers or aggregates only, with the real records held off-chain where they can be corrected or deleted
- every inflow is tagged at entry on three axes: domestic or foreign, corpus or general, and CSR or donation or membership fee
- foreign-tagged funds can never be routed to a partner NGO. The system must make this impossible, not merely discouraged
- a transfer to a partner NGO carries an automatic 15% ineligible-application tag — do not model it as a 100% pass-through of value
- the ledger must not split a ₹1,000 payment into a donation part and a membership-fee part
- the same rupee is never presented as both CSR-eligible and 80G-deductible
- you design to the legal constraints; you do not give legal or tax advice, and qualified professionals confirm them
- no token that could be traded or held as value is ever issued

### 9. Finding your approach

Ten teams left alone with the word blockchain will build ten token ledgers. Do not be one of them until you have argued the question properly.

Four questions decide a serious proposal. Does a chain earn its place here, against a signed append-only log with published hashes and an independent auditor? Who or what attests that a thousand meals were served, and why should anyone believe that source? Who holds the signing keys for a charity, and what happens when they are lost? And at ₹1,000 a membership, do per-transaction costs make your design uneconomic?

One thing to raise with the sponsor. The context brief treats 80G as making the ₹1,000 tax-attractive and mentions a “50% tax rebate” in the corporate pitch. Under India’s new personal tax regime, now the default, Chapter VI-A deductions including 80G are generally not available to individuals. Confirm this before you build any part of the story around it.

Atria Foundation, LoveAll Card. Contact given in the brief: contact@atriafoundation.org, +91 8971101055.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS, IT. The most demanding software problem in this pool.
- **Technical skills** — Distributed ledgers and smart contracts, cryptographic hashing and signatures, key management, systems design under regulatory constraint, privacy-preserving data design, payment flows.
- **Other skills** — Reading legislation and turning rules into system behaviour, arguing an architecture decision on evidence, explaining a technical design to a non-technical trustee.

## Brief 20 — Telling One Elephant from Another

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** CS & IT · EC · Sponsor to confirm · Publication standard

> People have fingerprints. Elephants do not. Managing conflict means knowing which animal came back.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, the charitable arm of Atria Group, described in Brief 12. One of its four pillars is Bio-Diversity, which it describes as promoting harmony across species. Its visible work there so far has been plantation drives.

This problem sits further from that existing work than the Foundation’s other briefs, and it has one hard dependency: identifying individual animals needs a photographic catalogue that somebody has already built. Where that comes from is the first thing to settle.

### 2. The situation

Wildlife management asks questions about individuals, not just species. Is one elephant responsible for most of the crop raiding in a valley, or fifty? Is the animal we translocated the one that came back? How many distinct individuals use this corridor?

Right now those questions are answered by researchers who know the animals personally, which does not scale and disappears when they move on.

### 3. What is going wrong

Without individual identification, conflict data describes a place rather than an animal. That changes what you can do about it. A single repeat individual is a management problem with a specific answer. Fifty animals passing through is a landscape problem with a different one. The data cannot currently tell you which you have.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Some species make this easy. Tigers have stripe patterns that work almost like barcodes. Elephants do not. Identification relies on a combination of features that are harder to read from a photograph: ear fold shape, notches and tears in the ear edge, depigmentation patches, tail and tail-brush condition, body scars, and tusks where present.

There is also a harder statistical problem underneath, which most projects skip. A system that matches every photograph to the closest animal in its catalogue will confidently mislabel an animal it has never seen. Recognising that a new individual is new is the part that matters and the part that is difficult.

### 5. What already exists

Read ElephantBook before you propose anything. It is a semi-automated, human-in-the-loop elephant re-identification system published at ACM COMPASS in 2021 and deployed with the Mara Elephant Project in Kenya. Note the word semi-automated: the researchers who built it kept a person in the loop deliberately.

Your genuine contribution is that it was built for African elephants. Asian elephants are a different identification problem — most females and many males are tuskless, so tusk shape, one of the strongest African cues, largely disappears. That gap is real, defensible and worth a paper.

Whether a photographic catalogue of identified individuals exists anywhere, how large it is, and whether it can be shared. This project lives or dies on that answer, so get it in week one.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- photographic data and existing individual identifications from somewhere. This is the difference between a real project and a public-dataset exercise
- Brief 11 works on the same animals by a different method, for a different sponsor. Talk to whoever takes it — there is more overlap than either brief admits

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A working re-identification system tested on real photographs, reported honestly. Two numbers matter and most projects only report the first:

- how often it correctly matches a photograph to a known individual
- how often it correctly says “this animal is not in the catalogue”

Plus the workflow around it: how a researcher confirms or rejects a match, and how a new individual gets added.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- peer-reviewed documentation standard: reproducible method, stated assumptions, honest error rates
- report the failure cases, including which animals it confuses and why
- location data for individual animals is sensitive and can assist poaching. It does not go into a public repository, a public model, or a demo
- a match is a suggestion to a researcher, never an automatic record

### 9. Finding your approach

Read ElephantBook before you propose anything, and notice that its authors kept a human in the loop when they could have automated it. Work out why.

Then answer what the standard approach hides: what your system does with a blurry photograph of an animal it has never seen, and how few photographs of a new individual are enough.

Atria Foundation, under its Bio-Diversity pillar.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS and Electronics. Strong computer vision content.
- **Technical skills** — Computer vision and re-identification, metric learning and embeddings, open-set recognition, working with small and imbalanced datasets, human-in-the-loop system design.
- **Other skills** — Reading and building on published research, scientific writing, reporting error honestly, handling sensitive data.

## Brief 21 — Counting What Lives in a Place

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** CS & IT · EC · Sponsor to confirm · Needs narrowing

> Every method of measuring biodiversity misses something. Nobody has one that misses less.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, described in Brief 12, under its Bio-Diversity pillar. The Foundation runs plantation drives and describes its aim there as promoting harmony across species. It has no way at present of saying what any of that has done to the life in a place.

### 2. The situation

Someone wants to know what species live in a given area — a forest patch, a farm, a stretch of riverbank. That question sits behind conservation planning, environmental clearance, restoration monitoring and corporate biodiversity reporting.

Several methods exist and each sees a different slice:

- **Camera traps** catch ground-dwelling mammals well and miss small, arboreal and nocturnal-silent species
- **Acoustic recorders** catch birds, bats, frogs and insects that call, and miss everything that does not
- **Environmental DNA** detects traces of many species from a water or soil sample, but needs a laboratory, sequencing, and a reference database that is incomplete for Indian species
- **Transect and plot surveys** are reliable and expensive in expert time
- **Citizen records** such as bird and nature-observation platforms are large and heavily biased toward attractive species near roads

### 3. What is going wrong

Each method gives you a list that reflects the method as much as the place. Combining them is not addition, because each one detects different species with different probability, so you cannot simply pool the lists and call it a survey.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Because it is a genuine open problem in ecology, not an engineering oversight. Estimating how many species are present when your detection is imperfect and varies by method is a live statistical research area. Anyone promising a complete answer is overselling.

### 5. What already exists

Brief 11 covers acoustic species classification with CWS, and an engineering student is already working on that classifier. This problem overlaps with it directly. Whoever takes this must coordinate with that team, or the two will duplicate each other.

A study site, any existing survey data, and access to recording or sampling equipment. Find out what is available before you choose your methods.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- public biodiversity databases and citizen-science records, which are freely available
- a real study site, and permission to place equipment on it
- laboratory access if you want to do any DNA work. Without a sequencing arrangement, environmental DNA is not open to you and you should not build a proposal around it

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

One real place, at least two methods, one defensible estimate with its uncertainty stated. That is a complete and honest project.

What matters is the combination. Showing how two imperfect methods can be joined into an estimate better than either alone, with the assumptions written down, is the contribution. A species list without a stated detection assumption is a list, not a survey.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- every estimate carries its uncertainty and its detection assumptions
- an absence of records is not evidence of absence, and the output must not imply otherwise
- location records for threatened species are withheld or made deliberately imprecise
- any fieldwork follows the permissions required for the site

### 9. Finding your approach

Photograph-and-identify platforms already exist and are very good. Rebuilding one is not a contribution.

The opportunity is in the unglamorous middle: taking data that already exists and turning it into an estimate a conservation planner would act on. That means being honest about what each method cannot see.

### 10. Your sponsor

Atria Foundation, under its Bio-Diversity pillar.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS and Electronics, with a strong pull for anyone interested in ecology or statistics.
- **Technical skills** — Statistical estimation under imperfect detection, combining heterogeneous data sources, species classification from images or audio, sensor deployment, data visualisation.
- **Other skills** — Reading ecological literature, stating uncertainty, resisting the urge to claim completeness.

## Brief 22 — Getting Coconuts Down

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** Mech · EC · Physical build · ~9 teams

> A 20-metre trunk with nothing to hold on to, and fewer climbers every year. This is the farm’s biggest problem.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, described in Brief 12. The Foundation’s farm names coconut harvesting as its single biggest operational problem.

### 2. The situation

Coconut palms grow tall, with a smooth unbranched trunk and the fruit at the very top. Harvesting means getting a person or a machine up there, cutting bunches, and getting down. Skilled climbers are scarce, ageing, and the work is dangerous.

The farm reports that the robots currently available do not work well on coconuts.

### 3. What is going wrong

Unharvested or late-harvested coconuts are lost income. Dependence on a shrinking pool of climbers is a risk the farm cannot control. And unlike most farm tasks, this one cannot be solved by simply hiring more people, because the skill takes years to acquire.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

It has been attempted seriously, and you should know the numbers before you propose anything.

**Amaran** is an unmanned robotic coconut tree climber and harvester built at Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, in development since 2014 under Rajesh Kannan Megalingam and published in IEEE in 2020. A ring-shaped body clasps trunks of varying diameter, eight wheels drive it up and around the trunk, it copes with trunks leaning up to 30 degrees, and a robotic arm with four degrees of freedom cuts the bunches. Its measured performance is the most useful fact in this brief. Amaran averages **21.9 minutes per tree, of which about 14 minutes is setup**. That works out to roughly 22 trees a day, against about 15 for a human climber before fatigue. Cost is described as the barrier to wider deployment.

The team’s own summary of the difficulty is worth repeating: no two coconut trees are the same.

### 5. What already exists

Amaran and the published work around it, including more recent double-ring climber designs. Read the papers. A proposal that reinvents a 2020 design without knowing it exists will not survive the scoping round.

The most important question on this brief: which machines has the farm actually tried, and how exactly did they fail? Cost, tree geometry, setup time — or were they never tried at all? The answer changes what you should build, so ask before you design.

Number of palms, height range, trunk lean and diameter, ground conditions between trees, harvest frequency, and current cost per tree. Go and measure.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- the Foundation’s own farm, which should be the easiest site access of any problem in this pool
- the people who currently harvest, who know exactly where the time goes
- a workshop, tools and a materials budget, none of which is guaranteed

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

Something that goes up a real tree on the real farm and brings coconuts down, tested more than once, with three numbers: time per tree against the current method, cost to build one, and how many of the farm’s trees your design can actually handle.

That last number is where most designs quietly fail. A machine that works on a straight 40 cm trunk and cannot manage a leaning one has solved a fraction of the problem.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- safety first, and this is not a formality. A machine at 20 metres that falls, or a cutting tool that drops, can kill someone. Design the failure case before the working case
- the tree is not damaged. A palm is a long-term asset and injuring the crown can end it
- low cost and locally repairable, since cost is exactly what stopped the existing solutions
- works on the farm’s real range of trunk diameters and leans, not one ideal specimen

### 9. Finding your approach

Read the Amaran work before you design anything, then look hard at one number in it. Setup takes roughly fourteen of its twenty-two minutes per tree, so only about eight minutes is climbing and cutting.

Making the climb faster barely moves the total. Most teams will optimise the wrong number. Decide which number you are attacking, and say so.

## Brief 23 — Reading a Plant’s Electrical Signals

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** EC · CS & IT · Sponsor to confirm · Read section 04 first

> Plants generate measurable electrical activity. Whether it tells you a plant is in trouble before you can see it is an open question worth answering.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, described in Brief 12. This problem sits across two of its pillars — Bio-Diversity and the farm work behind Brief 22 — and would be tested on the Foundation’s own crops.

### 2. The situation

By the time a farmer can see that a plant is stressed — wilting, yellowing, stunted growth — the damage has usually been happening for days. Water stress, disease, nutrient deficiency and heat all announce themselves late. Anything that gives warning earlier is worth money and worth water.

### 3. What is going wrong

Existing early-warning tools measure the environment rather than the plant. Soil moisture sensors tell you about the soil. Weather stations tell you about the air. Neither tells you what the plant is actually experiencing, and two plants in the same soil can be in very different states.

### 4. Why this problem needs care before you start

This problem is usually described as plant sonification: converting a plant’s electrical activity into audible music, and using that to understand plant health. Read that sentence carefully, because it contains two different things and only one of them is science.

**The electrical activity is real.** Plants generate measurable electrical signals — action potentials, variation potentials and system potentials — in response to wounding, drought, temperature change and other stresses. This is an established field with a growing research literature, and at least one company, Vivent Biosignals, commercialises plant electrophysiology specifically for stress detection. There is real work here.

**The music is not evidence.** Sonification is a way of presenting a signal, not a way of measuring one. Converting voltage readings into notes adds no information; it removes most of it, because the ear cannot resolve what the data contains. Consumer devices that turn plants into music, such as PlantWave, are musical instruments, and they do not claim to diagnose anything.

So the opportunity is genuine and the framing is upside down. The question worth answering is whether the electrical signal predicts stress before it is visible. Sound is at most a way of showing the answer to someone, and you should treat it that way.

This is also the problem in this pool where it is easiest to produce something that looks impressive at a demonstration and measures nothing. Do not do that. It is a worse outcome than failing honestly.

### 5. What already exists

A substantial research literature on plant electrical signalling, including recent reviews and a body of work on electrophysiology for crop production. Commercial plant electrophysiology sensing exists. Low-cost electrode and amplifier designs for plant signals are widely published, so the hardware is within reach of a student budget.

Which site would host an experiment and which crops it grows. Settle both before week three, because the experiment needs a baseline period before anything else can happen.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- published research and open hardware designs for plant electrode measurement
- plants, growing space, and the ability to control watering and conditions
- laboratory or greenhouse access, which makes a controlled experiment far easier

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A real experiment with a real answer, either way.

- instrument a set of plants of one species, with a control group kept healthy
- apply one known, controlled stress — withholding water is the easiest to do properly
- record electrical signals continuously, and record visible condition separately
- report whether the signal distinguished stressed from healthy plants, and crucially **how long before the stress became visible**

That last number is the entire value of the project. If the signal tells you two days early, you have something a farmer would use. If it tells you at the same moment the leaves droop, you have proved something useful and negative.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- a control group is required. Without unstressed plants measured the same way, you have no result
- any claim about health carries the number of plants, the effect size and how confident you are
- sonification, if you build it, is presented as an output. It is never offered as evidence
- a negative result reported clearly is a successful project. Manufacturing a positive one is not
- no claims about plant consciousness, emotion, communication or preference. None of these follow from an electrical measurement

### 9. Finding your approach

A device that clips onto a plant and plays generative music will look and sound wonderful, and will tell nobody anything about the plant.

Start from the experiment and treat the hardware as the means. If your proposal names what you will measure, against what control, and what result would prove you wrong, you are ahead of almost every team on this problem.

### 10. Your sponsor

Atria Foundation.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — Electronics primarily, with CS for the analysis. Genuine instrumentation and signal processing content.
- **Technical skills** — Biopotential measurement, electrode design, low-noise amplification, signal processing, time-series classification, experimental design and statistics.
- **Other skills** — Designing an experiment that can disprove your own hypothesis, reading scientific literature critically, separating a real effect from a compelling demonstration.

### 1. Who you would work with

InspireMinds works on behaviour change in young people, using games, stories, yoga, reflection and design thinking rather than lectures. Its method runs Learn, Experience, Reflect, Apply, Grow.

It has a real product suite: four illustrated books — CricketYogi teaches the eight limbs of yoga through cricket, Happy Yogi covers nervous-system regulation, Yogic Science links anatomy to sustainability, Wealthy Yogi builds money habits — plus a nutrition game and Swadhyaya, a 21-day self-inquiry journal. It reports reaching more than 2,500 children across 7 or more states.

### 2. The situation

All of that is built for children. InspireMinds now wants to work with colleges, and young adults are a different problem.

The need is real. InspireMinds cites Deloitte’s 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey, which found that 94% of Gen Z respondents in India rate on-the-job learning as the most impactful thing for career progression, and reports that a third of Indian Gen Z respondents experience frequent stress or anxiety during their studies.

### 3. What is going wrong

Two things, and the second is the more interesting one.

First, a nineteen-year-old will not engage with a picture book written for a ten-year-old. The method may transfer to college students. The material does not.

Second, and this is the real opening: **InspireMinds cannot yet show that behaviour changed.** Its strongest reported figures are that 91% gained the intended nutritional and wellness knowledge and 87% learned more than two food or nutrient concepts. Read those carefully. Both measure what someone *knows* after a session.

InspireMinds’ own first principle is that children can know what is right and still not do it. So its evidence measures the thing it says is not the problem. Closing that gap — showing a real, observed change in what people actually do — is the most valuable thing a student team could build for this organisation.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Because measuring behaviour is much harder than measuring knowledge, and much harder than it looks.

- people over-report good habits and under-report bad ones, so asking them is not enough
- almost any intervention works for three weeks. Novelty alone produces a result, and the interesting question is week seven
- campus life fights you: irregular timetables, exam cycles, peer norms and hostel routines all overwhelm an individual’s intentions
- observing behaviour without surveilling people is a genuine design constraint, not a formality

### 5. What already exists

The product suite, the five-stage pathway, and the pilot results above.

InspireMinds has also proposed a complete format for working with colleges — the Personal Growth Innovation Challenge, where each student picks a personal goal, diagnoses their own gap, and engineers a fix. It is thoughtfully designed. It is also a description of a competition rather than a problem to solve, so this brief takes the organisational need underneath it instead. See section 07.

The product materials, and the pilot data behind the 91% and 87% figures — how they were measured, and on how many people. Ask for both. The second one tells you what InspireMinds can and cannot currently prove.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- InspireMinds’ behavioural frameworks, curriculum toolkits and facilitation, all of which they have offered
- a study population sitting on your own campus, which no other brief in this pool has
- a campus cohort, a room and a timetable slot, and a way of recruiting participants. All of it is yours to organise
- a facilitation session from InspireMinds during your cycle, if you ask for one
- whoever at Atria approves a study involving students as participants. Find them in week one, because nothing can run without them

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

**An important note first.** InspireMinds pitched a campus challenge in which every student picks their own personal goal. That format cannot run inside this programme: with around nine teams each pursuing a different self-chosen goal, there is nothing comparable for a sponsor to greenlight, nothing to hand over at the end, and the evidence a student would carry away is a claim about themselves.

The version in this brief keeps InspireMinds’ real need and needs their agreement: **design an intervention that changes one specific behaviour in 18 to 22 year olds, run it on a real campus cohort, and measure honestly whether it worked.** Every team then attacks the same target from a different angle, InspireMinds can choose between the approaches, and the winning intervention becomes part of their college offering.

Choose one behaviour and name it precisely. Sleep timing. Screen use after midnight. Eating breakfast. Exercise three times a week. One.

Deliver: the intervention, a measured before-and-after with a comparison or baseline period, and a plain statement of what happened.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- **measure behaviour, not knowledge and not intention.** What someone learned, or plans to do, is not a result
- self-report alone is not enough. Pair it with something observable
- you need a baseline period or a comparison group. Without one you cannot claim anything
- every participant consents, knows they can stop, and is never identifiable in what you hand over. They are your classmates, so the pressure to take part is real and you must design it out
- stay out of clinical territory. If a participant discloses serious distress, you must have a route to a real person who can help, not a form and not a chatbot
- **report what actually happened.** An intervention that did not work, reported clearly, is a successful project. Manufacturing a positive result is the one failure that matters here
- you do not diagnose or screen anyone

### 9. Finding your approach

There are thousands of habit trackers and their documented problem is that people stop opening them. Assume yours will too, unless you can say why not.

Two questions decide whether a pitch is serious. What makes someone do the thing on day forty, when the novelty has gone and nobody is watching? And how do you know they did it, without taking their word for it and without following them around?

### 10. Your sponsor

InspireMinds.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS and IT for the intervention and data, Electronics for sensing and wearables. Open to any branch with an interest in behavioural science or statistics.
- **Technical skills** — Experiment design, measurement instrumentation, mobile and wearable data capture, time-series and statistical analysis, privacy-preserving data handling, interface design for daily use.
- **Other skills** — Research ethics and consent, running a study with human participants, recruiting and retaining them, reporting a result that disappoints the sponsor.

## Brief 25 — Ten Rupees a Ride

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** CS & IT · EC · Thin context · ~9 teams

> Twenty-three million people travel by train in India every day, most of them with a phone, no signal and hours to fill.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, described in Brief 12. This is one of a group of problems the Foundation has put forward that sit outside its four existing pillars.

Be aware that the brief is built on one paragraph of intent. Almost everything below the situation is yours to establish.

### 2. The situation

Indian Railways moves roughly 23 million passengers a day. A long-distance journey runs for hours. A suburban commute runs twice a day, every day, for years.

Mobile signal along a railway line is patchy and drops out entirely for long stretches. So passengers either download something before they board, or stare out of the window.

### 3. What is going wrong

Nothing is broken. This is an opportunity that nobody has taken at a price ordinary passengers would pay.

The target set for you is ten rupees per person per ride. That number is the whole problem. It rules out almost every business model that works elsewhere, and it is low enough that a passenger might actually pay it without thinking.

### 4. Why it is not already fixed

Two reasons, and only one of them is technical.

The technical one is that you cannot stream from the internet on a moving train, so content has to already be on board. Then one server has to serve hundreds of phones at once over wireless, in a metal tube, while everyone is watching something different. Wireless capacity, not storage, is what breaks first.

The commercial one is harder. At ten rupees a head you cannot license premium film content. The rights alone would eat the fare several times over. Indian Railways has announced its own on-demand video, news and multilingual content on trains, and edge-cached video for rail operators is already a commercial product internationally, so you would not be first.

### 5. What already exists

Railway station wireless networks, Indian Railways’ own announced onboard entertainment, and international rail streaming products that cache content at the edge. Find out what each of them charges and what content they carry before you assume there is a gap.

Whether the Foundation has any relationship with a railway operator, and whether this has been discussed with anyone at Indian Railways, is for you to find out.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- your own hardware and a coach-sized space to simulate in, since a real train is unlikely to be available to you
- access to a train, a depot or a railway operator, if you can get it. Without one this is a laboratory project — a legitimate outcome, but a different one

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

A working system serving many devices at once from a local cache with no internet, and a costed argument that the whole thing survives at ten rupees a head.

The demonstration matters less than the number. Show how many simultaneous viewers one unit supports, what it costs to build and run, what content it could legally carry at that price, and how many passengers would have to buy for it to pay for itself.

Test at real scale. Thirty phones in a room is a result. Three is not.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- no content is distributed without the right to distribute it. A system that only works by ignoring copyright is not a system
- the design assumes no internet on board, not slow internet
- nothing is proposed for installation on rolling stock without acknowledging that railway equipment needs operator approval and certification
- the ten rupee figure is a constraint to be tested, not an assumption to be quietly dropped. If your model only works at fifty rupees, say so and show the arithmetic

### 9. Finding your approach

Most teams will treat this as a streaming problem and build a media server. That part is close to a solved problem, and it will not distinguish you.

The questions that decide it: how many phones can one wireless access point actually serve before the video stutters, and what changes if everyone watches the same thing at the same time rather than different things? What content can you lawfully carry for ten rupees a head, and who would want it? And who pays — the passenger, an advertiser, or the operator?

### 10. Your sponsor

Atria Foundation.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — Electronics for the wireless and hardware side, CS and IT for the software and the caching. One of the more balanced problems in this pool.
- **Technical skills** — Wireless networking and capacity planning, local caching and content delivery, adaptive streaming, embedded and low-power hardware, load testing at realistic scale.
- **Other skills** — Unit economics, content licensing basics, reading a regulated environment, testing a claim rather than demonstrating a feature.

## Brief 26 — Work After Sixty

**Sponsor:** Atria Foundation  
**Tags:** CS & IT · Thin context · Read section 04 · ~9 teams

> Thirty years of experience walks out of the building on a Friday afternoon and never comes back. Almost nobody has worked out how to use it.

### 1. Who you would work with

Atria Foundation, described in Brief 12. Its Education pillar already runs skilling and placement through the Atria Mega Skill Centre, which has trained over 4,000 people and placed around 620 with employers. That is the same shape of problem at the other end of a working life.

As with Brief 25, the brief is built on one paragraph of intent. Establish the rest yourself.

### 2. The situation

People in India’s organised sector typically retire around 58 to 60, and then live for decades. Many of them are highly skilled, have run teams, solved problems and know an industry from the inside.

Some want to keep working. Not all of them on the old terms: fewer hours, no commute, no travel, something that fits around health or caring for a spouse.

### 3. What is going wrong

That experience mostly goes unused. The people who want work cannot find the right work, and the constraints they come with — part-time, remote, flexible, no heavy travel — are exactly the ones ordinary job platforms handle worst.

### 4. Why this problem needs care before you start

The obvious reading is that no platform exists. That reading is wrong, and finding out why matters more than anything else you will do in the first fortnight.

Job portals for retired people in India already exist. Some have run for years. Several were built by retired people themselves. Go and look at them, sign up, see how many live vacancies each one actually carries, and work out what happened.

What you will probably find is that this is a two-sided market that fails on the side nobody builds for. Retirees are easy to recruit; you could fill a database in a week. Employers willing to hire a 64-year-old part-time, with accommodations, are not easy to find, and no amount of interface design produces them. The barrier is that employers do not want to, and a platform does not fix that.

So the question underneath this brief is not how to build a matching platform. It is what would actually change an employer’s mind — or whether paid employment is the right target at all, when mentoring, teaching, advisory work and community roles may use the same experience with none of the same resistance.

### 5. What already exists

Several Indian job portals aimed at retired people and the over-sixties. Treat them as your literature review, not your competition.

Whether the Foundation can open a group of retired people, or its Skill Centre employer relationships, to you. Ask early: the employer side is the hard half.

### 6. What you would have to work with

- retired people you can talk to, who are not hard to find and who will tell you things a survey never would
- the Foundation’s existing employer relationships through the Skill Centre, if they can be opened to you
- an employer willing to take part in a trial during your cycle. Finding one is arguably the project

### 7. What done looks like in 90 days

Evidence about the demand side, and something built on top of it.

The strongest possible outcome is small and real: a handful of genuine matches that happened, with the story of what made each employer say yes. Ten real placements beats a platform with a thousand registered profiles and no vacancies.

If you conclude the platform is not the answer, say so and show what is. That is a finding, not a failure.

### 8. Rules you cannot break

- talk to employers in the first two weeks. A team that has only spoken to retirees by week six has built on the easy side of the market
- no personal data about any individual is published or shared without their clear agreement
- no promise of work to anyone during a student project. Raising the hopes of someone looking for income is a real harm
- design around the constraints people actually state, not the ones you assume older people have

### 9. Finding your approach

A profile database with a matching algorithm is where almost every team will start, and it is the thing that has already been built several times.

Two questions decide whether your pitch is serious. Where does the demand come from — what makes an employer post a role they would not otherwise have posted? And what is the smallest version of this that could produce one real match inside your cycle, so you find out whether any of it is true?

### 10. Your sponsor

Atria Foundation.

The programme team will put you in touch and set up the first conversation.

### 11. Skills and branches

- **Branch** — CS and IT. Suits students who want product and research work more than heavy engineering.
- **Technical skills** — Matching and recommendation logic, accessible interface design for older users, lightweight backend, privacy-aware data handling.
- **Other skills** — Interviewing on both sides of a market, testing a demand assumption before building, accessibility, reporting a conclusion the sponsor did not expect.
